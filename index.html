Deciphering central nervous system tumors for better diagnosis and treatment

We investigate how cancer genomes evolve, how oncogenic alterations reshape the tumor microenvironment, and how these changes influence disease progression and treatment response. By integrating spatial and single-cell genomics, 3D genome organization, and patient-derived tumors, we connect discoveries about genome structure to clinically relevant questions.

Research

Cancer genome evolution and spatial tissue organization are intertwined. We combine patient samples, advanced sequencing, imaging, and computational methods to understand how genomic alterations change tumor biology.

ecDNA, oncogene amplification and cancer evolution

How does the architecture of oncogene amplification influence tumor evolution and response to therapy?

We study extrachromosomal DNA (ecDNA), homogeneously staining regions, and other amplification structures in gliomas and brain metastases. By integrating DNA FISH, whole-genome sequencing, and spatial transcriptomics, we investigate how amplification architecture relates to tumor cell states, immune organization, and clinical outcomes.

Key work: ERBB2 amplification in esophageal adenocarcinoma brain metastases

  • ecDNA
  • ERBB2 and EGFR
  • DNA FISH
  • Brain metastases
  • Therapy response

Spatial ecosystems of brain tumors

Which oncogenic drivers reshape the cells around them, and where?

We use spatial transcriptomics and single-cell profiling to study gliomas in their native tissue context. Integrating these maps with genomic alterations reveals how oncogenic drivers relate to malignant cell states, immune populations, vasculature, and spatial neighborhoods across primary and recurrent tumors.

Key work: Oncogenic drivers shape the tumor microenvironment in human gliomas

  • Spatial transcriptomics
  • Single-cell RNA and ATAC
  • Tumor microenvironment
  • Glioma

Treatment-driven evolution and recurrence

How do treatment and acquired mutations reshape recurrent gliomas?

We study longitudinal tumor genomes, temozolomide-associated hypermutation, mismatch-repair alterations, and changes in malignant cell states after therapy. Our goal is to connect the evolutionary history of recurrent tumors with their changing spatial ecosystems and identify vulnerabilities that emerge during progression.

  • Longitudinal genomics
  • TMZ hypermutation
  • DNA repair
  • Glioma recurrence

Chordoma evolution and treatment resistance

What keeps TBXT switched on in chordoma, and which tumors will come back after treatment?

Chordoma is a rare tumor of the skull base and spine driven by the developmental transcription factor TBXT (brachyury). We are building a multi-omic atlas of chordomas from the skull base, mobile spine and sacrum, including tumors sampled at several points during treatment.

By integrating single-cell, genomic and spatial profiling, we study how chordomas are regulated, how they evolve, and how they respond to therapy.

  • TBXT (brachyury)
  • scATAC-seq
  • Long-read WGS
  • Spatial transcriptomics
  • Radiation therapy

Chromatin architecture and mutation

How does the folded genome decide where mutations land?

Two meters of DNA fold into a nucleus a few micrometers wide, organized into self-interacting domains (TADs) that control gene regulation and DNA replication. We showed that somatic mutation rates track this 3D structure across cancers. We now use Hi-C and single-cell chromatin accessibility in brain tumors to connect genome folding with DNA damage, repair and tumor evolution.

Key work: Nature Genetics 2020

  • Hi-C
  • scATAC-seq
  • Mutational processes
  • Epigenome

Complex rearrangements and tumor evolution

What do shattered chromosomes do to gene regulation, and does it matter for patients?

Catastrophic rearrangements rewire which genes sit next to which enhancers. We develop methods that use chromatin organization and long-read whole-genome sequencing to reconstruct rearranged chromosomes and predict the regulatory changes they cause, and we follow these changes from primary tumors to recurrence and brain metastasis.

Key work: Nature Genetics 2020

  • Long-read WGS
  • Structural variants
  • Brain metastasis
  • Clinical genomics

Tumors we study

Our questions begin with patient tumors and are shaped by collaborations across neurosurgery, neuropathology, oncology, genomics, and computational biology.

Glioblastoma and gliomas

The most common malignant brain tumors in adults, and the focus of our spatial and single-cell work.

Chordoma

A rare tumor of the skull base and spine with few treatment options and little genomic data.

Brain metastases

We investigate how metastatic cancers, including esophageal adenocarcinoma and HER2-positive breast cancer, evolve and adapt within the brain.

Other CNS tumors

Rare and pediatric tumors where integrative multi-omics can sharpen diagnosis.

Alumni

    Join us

    We're looking for curious people who want to turn large, messy patient datasets into biological insight. We train lab members across computational and experimental approaches and support each person's next career step.

    Postdoctoral fellows

    Develop and apply algorithms for spatial transcriptomics, single-cell chromatin, Hi-C and long-read genome data from primary brain tumors. You'll work on tumor behavior, clinical outcomes and resistance to therapy, and build your own research program.

    Email Kadir with your CV, a short note on your background, how our interests intersect and your timeframe. Informal inquiries are welcome.

    See the job posting

    PhD and rotation students

    We are part of the MD Anderson UTHealth Houston Graduate School of Biomedical Sciences (GSBS). Apply to the graduate program rather than directly to the lab; first-year students rotate through about three labs before choosing a thesis lab.

    We usually host rotation students every quarter. Prospective students are welcome to get in touch with questions.

    Undergraduate and high school students

    We host students through the CPRIT-CURE Summer Undergraduate Program and the PCCSM program for STEM-focused high school and college students and first-year medical students.

    We especially welcome students from groups underrepresented in science and medicine. Contact Kadir about open positions.

    How we mentor

    • Every trainee owns a project with a clear question, and gets regular one-on-one time to shape it.
    • We write code that others can read and rerun, and we share methods and data openly.
    • Computational and clinical collaborators sit at the same table, so analyses stay close to patients.
    • We plan careers early, whether the next step is academia, industry or medicine.
